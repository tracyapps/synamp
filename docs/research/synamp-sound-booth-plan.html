Chapter 01

The verdict, and the reframe

Yes — this is worth doing. But not as first imagined: the design pointed a population instrument at a single-user system and never stated whose parameter each experiment estimates, and that single unexamined assumption is the reason the original plan fails. Named honestly, the idea decomposes into three separately-judgeable projects — an owner-first local study to run first, a small screened validation panel, and a public dataset booth that must re-earn its launch on different grounds than the ones it was sold on. The strongest idea in the plan needs none of them: synthesize the audio yourself, and ground truth arrives by construction.

The question the plan never asked

Every calibration experiment estimates a parameter, and every parameter is defined by the distribution it is drawn from. The compiler parses your prompts and thresholds your library, so what it needs are your operating points — where "chill" starts for you, what your vocabulary means, what you keep and what you skip. A public booth samples strangers, whose operating points come from a different distribution. More of the wrong distribution does not converge on the right parameter; it converges faster on the wrong one.

The field knew this before the plan was written. Music-emotion research introduced "bag-of-users" models in 2009 precisely to accommodate individual differences, and personalization is still an active line. The plan cites none of it and designs as if one aggregate were a valid target.

The reverse also holds, and the plan missed that too. Some questions genuinely are population questions: whether an analyzer metric tracks a percept at all; how noisy the task is for humans in general; whether your renders sound synthetic to naive ears — where you are the worst rater available, because you cannot hear your own generator's tells. The failure was never "crowds are useless". It is that the plan never split its constructs by whose distribution answers them, and so priced every question as if the crowd answered all of them.

Three projects, not one

Once each estimand is named, "the sound booth" stops being one project and becomes three — different customers, different costs, different kill criteria. The ordering is the review's cheapest finding: the first project falsifies or confirms the whole premise for the price of two weekends, before any booth code exists.

Ground truth by construction

The best idea in the plan is not the booth. When you synthesize the audio, the labels you would otherwise beg strangers for arrive free and exact: key, mode, chords, tempo, swing ratio, microtiming offsets, instrumentation, dynamics, brightness — known by construction, because you set them before rendering. Stimuli are unlimited; no humans are needed to train the objective half; no copyright exception is needed, because nothing borrowed is copied. Published perception work reaches for the same trick: PROMS rendered its stimuli from MIDI precisely to escape the timing, timbre and intensity inconsistencies of human performers observed.

Build this half whether or not any booth ever launches. It is the analyzer's validation substrate and the training corpus for the objective constructs the dossier flagged as lacking permissive models. Two honest limits: exact parameters are not percepts — whether a parameter maps to a percept is exactly what projects 1 and 2 exist to measure — and construction says nothing about render realism, which is the synthetic-to-real trade priced in the callout below and re-examined in c2.

What the original design got wrong

  • The estimand. Never named. The plan's own value claim — "calibrates the metric→percept mappings that the compiler needs" — slides between your compiler's thresholds (yours alone) and community-level construct validity (a crowd question) without noticing they are different parameters, drawn from different distributions.
  • Three instruments at once. Forced choice, sliders and tags were imagined co-equally, with no construct→instrument map; the research's own first rule is one instrument per construct. Tags have no path into the compiler's predicate space, and the microtiming module as designed measures device-polluted detectability — an upper bound valid for nobody's compiler. Detail in c2.
  • The unpriced synthetic-to-real trade. Free copyright compliance was treated as free validity. The closest music analogue, Slakh2100, overfits — models trained on it alone generalize poorly to real recordings — and TTS-trained keyword spotters learn generator artifacts and lose accuracy on real speech, both observed. Whether rendered clips evoke the same affective judgements as real records has no study behind it in this pass. The price was never measured; it was assumed cheap.
  • The funnel arithmetic. Plan on roughly 35–50% of started sessions being usable; one strong module needs ~100–150 finished sessions; three modules split the same traffic into three underpowered datasets; and 120–200 usable sessions implies ~400–500 visits — traffic the plan generates by hoping. Illustrative arithmetic from the research's own numbers, marked candidate; worked out in c4.
  • The missing competitor. Your own skip/removal/repeat stream is free, in-distribution, and produced by the exact consumer the compiler serves. A three-option "why did you skip" logger is one day's work and out-produces the booth for personal calibration. The plan contains no implicit-feedback arm at all.

Chapter 02

Which constructs actually need other people

The adversarial review found the plan’s central flaw in one sentence: it never said whose parameter each experiment estimates — it proposed a population instrument to serve a single-user system. The rule that decides everything here: for each construct, name whose parameter you are estimating and from whose distribution — then the instrument follows. Stretching one instrument, or one crowd, across several kinds of question is the main way a public test produces unusable labels.

Why the rule is decisive. An instrument estimates a parameter. If the compiler needs your operating point on a metric, strangers’ ratings are not a noisy estimate of it — they estimate a different one. More of the wrong distribution does not converge on the right answer; it converges faster on the wrong one. candidate

The per-construct verdict

Run the rule across every construct SynAmp consumes and the verdicts split three ways: population prior needed (only a small, screened external panel produces the target), own ratings sufficient (you rating stimuli against generator-known parameters — licit, exact, cheap), and neither needed (measured or published, not judged). The table is the centrepiece; the two sharpest rows follow.

The two sharpest verdicts

Valence: not a noisy target — a different target

Valence is the least agreed construct in every dataset this research pass opened: α = 0.222 in the screened-crowd ranking study, against 0.360 for arousal (ISMIR 2017), and 0.364 on the citizen-science platform, against 0.505 for arousal and 0.192 for tags (Páez et al. 2021). observed By the field’s own bands, α below 0.667 means discard the layer (Krippendorff’s α verdict bands). For SynAmp the consequence is stronger than noise: if the playlist logic needs your pleasantness operating point, a population mean is not a low-precision estimate of it — it is a precise estimate of a different quantity, mass-taste valence. More strangers buy precision on the wrong number. If SynAmp wants valence at all, the labels must come from you.

Microtiming: a threshold valid for nobody’s compiler

A pooled public “detection threshold” is an upper bound, polluted twice: by devices and by naivety. The headphone gate alone may discard about a third of online arrivals (cumulative pass rate 64.7% in Woods et al. 2017), and untrained listeners in uncontrolled contexts need larger deviations before they notice anything. observed The number you would publish is not your threshold or even the crowd’s — it is the weakest surviving detector’s, on whatever hardware he happened to use. The compiler needs your threshold on your playback chain, and the only cheap route is a staircase you run yourself: a 60–80-trial 2AFC on your own rig. candidate For scale (candidate arithmetic): the pooled-booth alternative needs roughly 430–880 finished trials for a ±10 % psychometric curve — and still returns a number belonging to nobody. The review’s estimate of the trade: a 60-trial local staircase beats 400 pooled stranger trials.

Instrument per construct

Once construct and estimand are named, the instrument mostly follows. The original design fixed three response formats first and never mapped them to constructs — the measurement pass’s first rule, violated. Compressed:

Two points must survive compression. First, tags scored the worst agreement in the closest precedent — α = 0.192 on the citizen-science platform, below the 0.667 discard line — and the output-agreement games that built earlier tag datasets rewarded common tags and dropped rare, verbose ones by design (Law et al. 2009). observed Tags are retrieval-friendly side labels, not measurements. Second, and decisive: a stranger’s tag vocabulary has no path into the compiler’s predicate space. The compiler consumes predicates over analyzer metrics; a stranger’s “dreamy” enters only if the vocabulary is derived from the compiler itself — your prompts and corrections. That is an owner-labelling job, not a booth.

The honest limits

Four constraints bound any version of this — cheap to accept now, expensive to discover later.

The loudness confound — a hard rule, not a calibration

Loudness is among the seven psychoacoustic features that carry most of second-by-second emotion reports (Coutinho & Dibben 2013). observed If clips play at native levels, part of every “more energetic”, “more pleasant” or “better” vote is simply a louder vote, and apparent agreement rises artificially. The fix is mechanical: normalise each clip to one fixed measured loudness target (−23 LUFS broadcast-style or −16 LUFS streaming-style; the number matters less than its being one number; EBU R 128 practice), verify true peak ≤ −1 dBTP, log the BS.1770 measurement per clip, and allow no per-item level adjustment on the page (the standard permits only a narrow user gain, ≈±4 dB; BS.1534-3). observed Without this rule, the booth ships a loudness experiment wearing an energy costume.

Ten seconds is not a track

The booth labels 8–12-second static renders; the compiler queries aggregates over 4-minute recordings. Two untested assumptions bridge that gap: (i) a judgement on a segment transfers to the whole track, and (ii) a judgement on a render transfers to a recording. Nothing in the research pass tests either. Worse, emotional annotation needs an initial orientation time — a median of about 8 s for valence and 12 s for arousal before continuous ratings stabilise (Aljanaki et al. 2017) observed — so a 10-second clip is mostly orientation window, with the affect barely begun. Real tracks also have arcs, intro through chorus, that a uniform 10-second loop cannot carry; where naturalistic emotion work cares, it runs excerpts up to 61 seconds (Music Perception 2018, snippet-level only). unverified Short clips remain defensible for quality-style work; presenting their labels as whole-track truth is not.

The timing JND you will actually get

Lab-ideal timing discrimination is about 6 ms (and 2.5 % of the inter-onset interval above 240 ms) for a single perturbed tone in an isochronous sequence (Friberg & Sundberg 1995) observed — a figure quoted everywhere as if it applied to music. It does not. In realistic musical contexts the studies compiled in Senn et al. 2017 needed roughly 15–60 ms of displacement before effects appeared: a ±25 ms snare dose–response curve, bass-versus-drums shifts of 10–30 ms that listeners barely noticed, and effects only at ±31–62 ms in another. observed Unknown devices push it further: thresholds in a regular listening context are likely to differ from the JND. Never claim a personal threshold from a booth run; treat any pooled public figure as an upper bound and measure your own on your own chain.

Perceived or induced — decide before any slider is drawn

“Relaxing↔energizing” straddles two different measurement targets: perceived emotion (what the music expresses) and induced emotion (what it does to you). The annotation checklist demands an explicit flag for which one you measured; the original plan’s wording — “report what they perceive” — leaves it undefined. observed For SynAmp the distinction is not academic: the compiler’s goal is closer to induced-for-you (what should this track do to me right now), while a public booth can only collect perceived-from-strangers — a different parameter from a different distribution. Decide before drawing the slider, or you average two constructs into one that exists nowhere.

What is worth a first run — and what to strike

Run the verdicts together and the first-run list is short. Worth doing, in order:

  1. Construct validity for the analyzer’s flagship metrics, on a curated stimulus set, with 20–60 screened raters — the one irreplaceable use of other people, answered at a fraction of a public site’s cost. candidate
  2. “Sounds synthetic / cheap” with five to ten honest friends — the construct where other ears genuinely beat yours, needing no website. candidate
  3. Human reliability ceiling from the same small panel — agreement per layer, so you can tell model error from task noise. candidate
  4. Energy / arousal direction from your own ratings plus literature defaults — cheap, and it calibrates the one construct whose agreement is tolerable.

Strike from any first version: loudness (meter it, never vote it); valence from the crowd (lowest agreement, wrong target); mood category words from strangers (idiolectal, no route into predicates); preference and keep-or-skip (your own usage answers it — the review’s one-day skip logger out-produces the booth); groove direction (published; read it); genre texture (skewed crowd, synthetic cues); microtiming thresholds from a pooled booth (upper bound, valid for nobody); and the tag layer as primary measurement. Several of these are not worth measuring at all — and none of the survivors needs the public. The people SynAmp actually needs are a screened panel of twenty to sixty, or five friends with working ears.

Chapter 03

Building it — in the right order

Three builds, escalating: a weekend you run on yourself, a pilot you run on five friends, and — only if both survive — a public booth. Each build buys the right to attempt the next, and each carries kill criteria written down before any data arrives.

The order is the argument. The review found this plan pointing a population instrument at a single-user system; the fastest test of whether the crowd is needed starts with the one rater the compiler actually serves: you. Build A can falsify the core premise in a private weekend; Build B is first contact with others and tests mechanics, not statistics; Build C is the product, written only for the future where both have returned their numbers.

Build A — the owner-first weekend study (start here; no website required)

Two weekends, nothing distributed, so no licences, no consent text, no hosting. Three parts: stimuli, ratings, staircase.

Stimuli

Generate 80–120 clips spanning the parameter grid for the four to six constructs the compiler consumes — energy, microtiming offset, brightness, density, swing ratio as the shortlist. Because you render them from your own templates, the parameters are exact by construction: the swing ratio and the millisecond displacement of a part are numbers you wrote, not estimates observed. Render about twenty clips twice — a permissive General-MIDI bank versus a higher-quality sampled path — the render-realism probe. MuseScore_General (MIT) serves one side; VSCO 2 CE (CC0) the other confirmed. How much a synthetic render biases affective judgements is unknown — no quantitative study surfaced in the research pass unverified — which is exactly why the probe exists. Normalise every clip to one fixed, measured loudness target before anything is rated; uncontrolled loudness turns part of every "more energetic" slide into a loudness vote observed.

Ratings

Rate the full set in a local page — jsPsych is enough — with banded sliders (five labelled bands, not a bare line), one hidden duplicate per session, and anchors at unmistakable extremes. Two to three hours covers the set. The duplicates give you your own test–retest reliability; the anchors calibrate the scale and expose the compression that MUSHRA treats as grounds for invalidating a naive panel's results. One question per screen, randomised order, no previous scores shown.

Staircase

Then a 60–80-trial two-alternative forced-choice staircase on your own playback chain for the microtiming threshold: the same clip, one instrument displaced by d ∈ {0 (catch), ±15, ±30, ±45, ±60} ms, counterbalanced, verified on the render to move timing only — never level or phase. The published range: roughly 6 ms for sparse isochronous tones in the lab, 15–60 ms for deviations to matter in realistic music (Senn et al. 2017) observed. Your threshold lives in the second band, and only your own chain can measure it; pooled stranger thresholds are an upper bound valid for nobody's compiler.

Four read-outs, four decisions:

What a pass buys. A working instrument, a validated private corpus, and the first compiler defaults — before any stranger or licence is involved. A fail is worth nearly as much: two weekends spent discovering a broken instrument before it silently corrupted anything downstream.

The parallel one-day job. Add a skip/removal logger with a three-option reason menu to the player. Skips and removals are free, in-distribution, and produced by the exact consumer the compiler serves — the calibration stream every later build has to be argued against, not around candidate.

Build B — the five-friend pilot (invite-only; still no public launch)

Build B is the first time anything meets a person who did not write it. Invite five to ten people through the full flow as a visitor would meet it: consent and licence-grant text, headphone gate, one module — the energy slider with anchors and gold items — funnel instrumented at every step. One module, eight to twelve clips, ten minutes: one interpretable signal, not three underpowered ones.

Read-outs. The visit-to-usable conversion rate; inter-person agreement on the shared constructs; and whether anyone finishes unprompted, without a message from you halfway through. Agreement has a realistic ceiling: Krippendorff's α around 0.36–0.51 for arousal — valence lower still — found again and again from gold-gated academic crowds to public citizen-science platforms reproduced. Energy is the likeliest construct to show anything; if it shows nothing, the premise has nothing to stand on.

Kill criteria. Usable-session conversion below about 30%, or energy agreement at noise, means the public-crowd premise is mis-sized. The response is to shrink to a small screened validation study with paid or curated participants — not to add more friends. And five to ten people cannot estimate reliability at all: an α from a friends panel has a confidence interval spanning "unusable" to "fine". This build is a mechanics test — do the texts parse, does the gate behave, does the funnel convert, does anyone care enough to finish.

Nothing in Build B needs the CDN, the abuse budget, or the brand work. Those belong to Build C and should not be written until B has returned a number.

Build C — the public booth (only if A and B survive)

Everything below is written for the future where Build A's instrument tracks its parameters and Build B's funnel converts. If either failed, stop here; the rest of this chapter is engineering you do not need yet.

The stimulus pipeline

Three rules, each retiring a specific way the dataset breaks.

Timing is baked into files. Online playback scheduling carries 5–10 ms of inter-trial jitter against sub-millisecond lab clocks observed; any gap, overlap, or displacement a construct depends on is rendered into the audio itself, never scheduled live.

One loudness number. Every clip is normalised to a single measured target — −16 LUFS for streaming-style delivery or −23 LUFS broadcast-style, one of them and never a blend — with true peak ≤ −1 dBTP, a per-clip measurement log, and rejection of outliers beyond ±0.5 LU candidate. The number is stated in the manifest and on the methods page (EBU R 128 is the reference).

A hash-versioned manifest. Every stimulus carries a content hash (SHA-256); the manifest records the hashes; every response stores the hash of the exact audio it judged; stimulus sets are immutable inside a collection window candidate. The review called the failure this prevents "the failure with no alarm": if a render changes mid-collection — a bank update, a normalisation tweak, a fixed bug — and the manifest does not, responses attach to the wrong audio. No validator flags it, no screen fails, and the corpus is quietly wrong. The hash turns silent corruption into a loud one.

The public surface

The booth is a product surface, not a document. This plan, like the dossier, wears a paper system; the booth must wear SynAmp's clothes instead. The brand spec (design/brand-spec.md in the repo, itself sourced from the product's tokens) is the design system: near-black surfaces (#0e0e10 page, #16161a panels), a single accent — amber, #e0a33e — reserved for the live element (the clip that is playing, the armed control), hairline structure at 8% white, never pure black or white confirmed. The spec's own reading: restraint plus one warm LCD light. A booth styled as a research instrument recruits researchers; one styled as the product recruits listeners.

Hosting: never the NAS

Never serve a public audio site from the NAS. A home uplink melts at single-digit concurrent listeners, and the failure would arrive exactly when a share made the booth matter; worse, it puts a private stack's attack surface on the open internet candidate. The shape that works: static clips and a thin submission endpoint on a CDN or free static tier, the NAS behind nothing public. The audience must not flow in; only the artifacts — manifest, labels, aggregate reports — flow back.

The ethics package — launch conditions, not aspirations

The review tested this design against the critique that animates the whole idea — the reCAPTCHA analogy — and found it clears the core: nothing is gated behind the booth, so a visitor who declines loses nothing and nobody is coerced. What it does not clear is reciprocity: contributors currently receive nothing back. This list fixes that. These are conditions; the booth ships with all of them, or it does not ship.

  • Public release. Labels and stimuli are published as a public-domain (CC0) or CC-BY dataset: the act recasts from labour for a private tool into a donation to a commons.
  • Plain-language honesty, before any audio plays. The page says, in words a non-researcher reads: this trains a private, single-user tool you will not receive; your responses may train model heads; the data will be published under this licence. Ambiguity here — a teaser implying a product for them — is exactly where the extraction critique should land.
  • Feedback and credit. Every contributor gets a personal readout — their detection threshold, their slider profile — and the option of named credit in the dataset's contributor list.
  • An age gate. A stated minimum age and a policy for handling it, under counsel review. Minors are unaddressed in the current plan and must not be at launch.
  • Exclusion transparency. Headphone-gate and gold-item failure rates are published on the methods page, beside every screening rule. If a screen discards people, the discard rate belongs to the record.
  • Anonymous by design, honest about irreversibility. No accounts, no identity, submissions not linkable back to a person — and therefore not withdrawn once published. That sentence sits on the same screen as the licence grant.

One closing point is the ethics of a listening test, not of data. The test legitimately needs working ears — the gate is valid practice, and the numbers are the reality: about 35% of online arrivals fail the headphone check, and about 12% fail even the benign control (Woods et al. 2017) observed. What the site must not do is imply "anyone can do this" and then silently discard a third of everyone who tries. Failures get a neutral explanation — what the check was, why it matters, what to try instead — and the rates go on the public methods page. An honest gate filters; a half-open door that drops people mid-stride is what the critique warns about.

Adopt the list, and the ethics section stops being the plan's most aspirational part and becomes its strongest — the difference between a booth that reads as extraction on its worst day and one that is defensible on it. Engineering is iterable. Consent is not.

Chapter 04

Reading the data

Nothing has run yet, so this chapter is accounting for a booth that might: which standard to borrow, which raters to keep, how much agreement to expect, how to combine votes, and how many sessions the arithmetic actually costs. Source numbers carry observed; everything computed in this chapter is this plan's own candidate arithmetic.

The standard to steal from

The reference point is ITU-R BS.1534-3 — MUSHRA, the established recommendation for subjective audio assessment with a hidden reference and hidden anchors — with its stricter sibling BS.1116-3 for near-transparent differences. Their machinery exists because "this sounds better" is a noisy measurement, and they control the noise with structure rather than sample size: a hidden copy of the reference in every trial; hidden anchors at the extremes; banded scales; randomized order; and a post-screening rule that drops an assessor whose hidden reference is rated below a floor on too many items — below 90 on more than 15% of items disqualifies, and the inverted rule catches anyone who rates everything at the top. observed

What SynAmp should steal is the machinery, not the numbers. The 90 / 15% thresholds belong to a 0–100 quality scale with low-pass anchors; pasting them onto an emotion slider would be cargo cult. The translated tolerances below are this plan's own candidate values, to be recalibrated against your duplicates in the weekend study and pilot.

Rater quality: gates before averages

Three instruments decide whether a rater's data counts. Gold items ask questions with known answers: the closest precedent, a screened crowd ranking study, gated participants with a five-comparison gold quiz at an 80% pass mark, and kept in-task golds that warn and demote rather than silently poison. observed Instructional-manipulation checks catch people who never read the instructions — a validated filter that removes satisficers and raises statistical power. Duplicate items measure consistency directly: same clip, different context, deviation versus tolerance.

On failure, the default is weighting, not deletion: estimate per-rater accuracy from gold items and weight votes by it. Deletion discards signal, and the standard itself warns against over-aggressive exclusion — exclusion must be cautious, "bearing in mind the variability of assessors' sensitivities". observed Per-listener dossiers (agreement with others, repeatability, discriminability — the standard's own trio of listener metrics) belong in the booth as a reputation tier that modulates weight, not as a ban.

Agreement: the sobering baseline

Here is what music-emotion annotation actually produces, even under screening. A crowd of 823 annotators ranking clips for arousal and valence reached percent agreement of 0.839 and 0.801 — but chance-corrected agreement of only α = 0.360 and 0.222; the paper itself calls this "a fair level of agreement". A public citizen-science platform (189 users, 3,556 annotations) landed lower: α = 0.505 for arousal, 0.364 for valence, and 0.192 for discrete emotion tags. Percent agreement looks high; the chance-corrected coefficient — the number that matters — is low. observed

The interpretability bands are fixed: α at or above 0.800 is where reliable conclusions live; 0.667–0.800 is tentative; below 0.667 the convention is to discard the layer. For interval-valued slider data the parallel readout is ICC — poor below 0.40, good from 0.60 upward. State the consequence plainly: a layer below the tentative band must not be used to draw conclusions — not as a compiler default, not as a training target. On the published numbers, that disqualifies every valence and tag layer measured so far and puts even the screened arousal layer under 0.4. Chapter 2 draws exactly this line: valence and category words go to the owner, not the crowd.

Aggregation: votes, models, medians

Combining ratings is a decision per data type, not a default:

  • Majority vote only for categorical layers after triage — gates passed, at least three votes per item, errors roughly independent. It is what the screened crowd protocol used, and it is the weakest defensible option.
  • Model-based aggregation for noisy categorical labels: Dawid–Skene-style models give each rater an estimated confusion matrix and infer the true label jointly, so "right in general, biased toward intense" is modelled instead of flattened. Bayesian versions add per-label uncertainty and flag items that need more votes.
  • Sliders, model route: hierarchical models that absorb per-rater bias and scale-use — one rater's centre is another's 65 — once n permits.
  • Sliders, robust route: non-parametric summary as the workhorse — medians, IQR outlier examination, bootstrap confidence intervals — the reading the audio standard itself prescribes.

Power arithmetic

The following is arithmetic, not evidence: planning math under stated assumptions, for the pilot's own duplicates to check. Working assumption: σ ≈ 20 points between raters on a 0–100 slider; the heterogeneous public case (σ ≈ 25) doubles the raters needed for the same precision.

The funnel: what a run really costs

Every session passes a gauntlet before its data counts. The headphone gate alone removes about a third of online listeners — a cumulative pass rate of 64.7% across 5,154 participants observed — and it filters good listeners too: 12% fail even a benign control version. Attention and gold gates remove more; 3–28% misreport eligibility; completion and bot filtering skim again. Combined survival: roughly 35–50% of started sessions candidate.

Now run the friends-scale version, because it is the version that will actually happen first: 40–60 invites at 35–50% survival yields 15–30 usable sessions. With σ ≈ 20–25 and single-digit raters per clip, item confidence intervals sit near ±20 points on a 0–100 scale, and an agreement estimate's interval spans from unusable to fine. A friends-scale run cannot decide anything — not because friends are bad raters, but because twenty sessions is twenty sessions.

Design for it; do not discover it. One strong module needs roughly 100–150 finished sessions, and three modules split the same traffic into three underpowered datasets. That is why Chapter 3's pilot carries funnel instrumentation: it exists to replace these estimates with a measured conversion rate before anyone builds a booth for traffic that does not exist.

Into the brain: defaults with uncertainty

Nothing above produces facts about your library. What the booth can produce is a set of defaults with uncertainty for the prompt compiler: a per-construct prior — a direction and slope for energy, a detection band for microtiming, a reliability coefficient per layer — each stored with its session count, dispersion, and agreement statistic, so every default carries its own error bar. Read them the way a published effect size is read at the start of a new experiment: a starting point, with a confidence, not a finding.

Chapter 2 makes each compiler threshold ultimately the owner's operating point, so these numbers seed the thresholds; they do not govern them. Your own feedback — skips, removals, repeat plays, prompt corrections — comes from the right distribution and overrides the priors as it accumulates. Record a ceiling next to each prior: a model cannot beat the disagreement between human raters, so the measured α is the best target any analyzer can be validated against — the human-ceiling readout the closest published precedent ships for exactly this purpose. The booth's numbers, if they arrive, win the right to move a default. Nothing more, and nothing less.

Chapter 05

The licensing algebra

Does doing this training and testing yourself sidestep the licensing problems and make the product more attainable? Partly yes: the plan removes the two causes that forced a published team into a non-commercial release. Partly no: it cannot conjure a permissively licensed base encoder, and a clean licence does not make a model good.

What there is to escape

There is no permissively licensed pre-trained model for the constructs SynAmp needs most: categorical mood, trained danceability, production era, key detection. The strongest music-representation models carry non-commercial weights confirmed: MERT is CC BY-NC 4.0 (model card), the Essentia models are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (licence), and MusicGen puts MIT on the code but NC on the weights. The shelf is real, and poisoned for anything you intend to ship. The plan's escape hatch is to stop buying detectors and start making them: generate your own stimuli, collect your own labels, train small heads on frozen features. This chapter asks how far that hatch actually opens.

Two cases, one rule

The warning case

In 2026 a research team published Taste-aware music retrieval from audio embeddings (Spanio & Rodà, CBMI) with exactly SynAmp's proposed architecture: small heads trained on frozen audio-encoder embeddings. Their repository's licence section reads, verbatim (source, read 28 September 2026):

“Weights license: the trained heads are released under CC-BY-NC-4.0 (they derive from a non-commercial dataset and from MULE's CC-BY-NC weights). The code in this repository is Apache-2.0 (see License).”

Two clean causes blocked their heads from shipping: someone else's non-commercial training corpus, and a non-commercial encoder.

The contrast case

SynAmp's plan deletes both causes. The stimuli are generated downstream of a permissive chain — your score, rendered through open synthesizers whose licences do not reach their output — so the audio is yours. The labels arrive through an explicit contributor dedication, so they are yours. The frozen encoder can be picked from the permissive set. When every input is owned or permissive, the combination — a head you actually own, to publish, serve and relicense — follows.

observed The rule the two cases display: fine-tuning on non-commercial weights yields a non-commercial derivative. This is a published team's practice, not a court ruling — the legal question itself remains one for counsel.

observed The converse, which the contrast case exhibits: ownership is achievable when all inputs are permissive or owned.

The algebra below is read from licence texts and published practice, not legal advice; contested joints are collected as questions for counsel, not defaults for an implementer.

The asset table

The soundfont provenance trap

Permissive software does not imply permissive sounds. FluidSynth (LGPL-2.1) and sfizz (BSD-2-Clause) place no conditions on the audio you render — a program's output is not covered by the code's licence unless it constitutes a work based on the code itself (GNU GPL FAQ) confirmed — but a soundfont is a bundle of recordings, each with its own rights, and two of the most respected free banks document their own provenance gaps in their own files.

GeneralUser GS, from its own licence file: “Because GeneralUser GS originated as a personal project with no intention for publication, I cannot be 100% sure where all of the samples originated… This uncertainty may concern you if you intend to use GeneralUser GS in a commercial software product.” confirmed

Sonatina Symphonic Orchestra, from its README: “In the case of a few very old soundfonts I have no idea who the original authors were or what licensing might apply… as it is impossible for me to know the exact origin of everything.” confirmed

The safe pattern is binary: pure synthesis, where no samples exist at all (Surge XT, Vital, or your own code — every rendered byte unambiguously yours); or a bank whose licence you have read from its own file and which clears commercial use and distribution of renders. Confirmed in this pass: MuseScore_General.sf2 (MIT, from its own licence file), VSCO 2 CE (CC0), University of Iowa MIS (“used for any projects, without restrictions”) confirmed. Philharmonia allows commercial renders but not redistribution of the sample set itself — the render-versus-bank distinction; recording every bank's name, version and hash in the dataset manifest is what makes it auditable. Hardware-ROM-derived banks (the “SC-55” class) are presumed copyrighted and were not cleared in this pass unverified. One bad sample poisons the whole chain: render, dataset and model card alike.

What this closes — and what it does not

Closes.

  • Ownable heads. A mood, energy, danceability or production-era head trained on your labels over permissive frozen features is a weights file you can ship without an NC or SA shadow.
  • A publishable corpus. Stimuli, labels and recipe released licence-clean — a small public dataset that needed nobody's permission.
  • Objective labels by construction. Key, tempo, swing ratio, microtiming offsets, arrangement parameters: exact from the generator, needing no humans, no rights, no copyright exception.

Does not close.

  • The base encoder. You still need a permissive one, and none of them has the music-specialised fit of the NC models; you also cannot replace an NC detector whose training corpus you cannot reproduce.
  • Ecological validity. Labels on synthesized stimuli describe synthesized stimuli; transfer to real productions is unmeasured until the real-anchor comparison says otherwise.
  • Anything the synthetic-to-real gap taints. A licence-clean model can still be wrong: this work buys the right to ship, not quality.

Corollary: licence pollution is one-way. Keep any NC-encoder experiments in a separate, clearly marked internal corpus; a single NC source taints whatever release it enters.

Requires counsel

Four questions an engineer must not answer by choosing a default:

  • Whether shipping the numeric outputs of a non-commercial model — BPM, key, mood probabilities — is a derivative or commercial use of the model. CC's own FAQ: “This is hard to know without having all of the facts” (CC FAQ).
  • Whether a ShareAlike obligation attaches to model weights trained on ShareAlike-licensed embeddings or outputs — are weights “Adapted Material”? CC's guidance does not settle it.
  • Whether a public site's consent flow and the contributor grant are adequate: contract formation, inalienable moral rights, privacy and data-protection review, irrevocable dedication versus deletion requests, sui generis database rights.
  • Age and formation for young contributors: whether a minor can validly grant the licence at all, and what gate the flow requires if not.

The algebra in one line: what you build end to end is yours; what you borrow from a non-commercial source stays under review; and the base encoder is the one part of this plan that self-generation cannot make yours.

Chapter 06

The plan, and the decisions

This is the commit point. Chapters 1–5 were evidence; what follows is what to do, in what order, with what kill criteria, and which calls no research file can make for you. The sequence is built so the cheapest honest test always runs first — and the expensive thing only runs if the cheap ones pass.

The first move is not the booth

Before any stimulus pipeline, consent text, or booth code: build the skip/removal/repeat logger in the player, wired into the feedback tables the brain dossier already specifies, with a short reason menu on every skip:

  • wrong energy
  • wrong genre / vibe
  • not now — interrupted

One day of work. Every skip, removal, and repeat-play is a label produced by the exact consumer the compiler serves — free, continuous, and in-distribution; the reason menu turns a skip from an ambiguous "wrong for this occasion" into a calibratable signal. It outranks everything else on the board because it is the compiler's actual calibration stream, the thing the booth can only proxy; because it costs nothing and cannot fail the way an instrument can; and because once it exists, the booth's case must be argued against it rather than around it. Every hour on the booth now has to justify itself over an hour of real usage data. That is the honest test, and it costs a day.

The sequence

What the booth really costs

Priced honestly: stimulus design, build, and analysis for the public booth come to candidate ≈2.5–4 working weeks solo, spread over 4–8 calendar weeks, with counsel hours on top and recruitment sized to Ch. 4's funnel — 400–500 visits to net 120–200 usable sessions. Then the part plans never cost: the open-ended maintenance tail of operating a public service — hosting, security patching, log and complaint handling, per-release dataset packaging, indefinitely. Beside it stands the comparable alternative: the same weeks spent on steps 1–2 and on prompt-compiler eval work, which produce owner-visible value whether or not anyone ever visits a website. That asymmetry is the whole comparison. The booth's expected value is concentrated in the dataset and public-artifact ambitions — a legitimate second project, not the compiler's infrastructure — and if you build it, say so plainly in its own README, or the messaging fails the ethics package's honesty condition before launch.

What "the booth told us something real" means — pre-registered

Moving a threshold after seeing the data — up or down — converts a measurement into a rationalisation. The whole point of writing these numbers first is that future-you does not get a vote.

Decisions only the owner can make

The review flagged genuine file-level disagreements. They are not blendable; each changes what the result may claim.

The honest bottom line: the research underneath this plan is sound, the licensing architecture genuinely works — verified against a real published team whose non-commercial inputs forced non-commercial heads, the exact failure SynAmp's chain avoids — and the instruments are standard-backed. None of that rescues the original economics. The value is smaller and later than the pitch suggested, and most of it was never behind the booth at all: it sits in the owner's own ratings, the usage stream, and the by-construction labels that need no strangers. The weekend study is the cheapest possible way to find out whether what remains is real — and if it is not, you will have spent two weekends and one day learning that, instead of a month of evenings building a website for a parameter nobody asked for.
